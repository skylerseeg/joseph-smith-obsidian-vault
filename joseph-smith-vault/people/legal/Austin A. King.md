# ⚖️ Austin A. King

**Tags:** #person/foe #role/lawyer #era/missouri
**Born:** September 21, 1802, Sullivan County, Tennessee
**Died:** April 22, 1870, Richmond, Missouri
**Occupation:** Circuit Court Judge (Missouri), Governor of Missouri (1848–1852), U.S. Representative

---

## Summary

Austin A. King was the Missouri circuit court judge who presided over the Richmond Court of Inquiry in November 1838 — the legal proceeding that committed Joseph Smith and his companions to [[Liberty Jail]] on charges of treason and murder. His conduct of the hearing has been criticized by Mormon historians as politically motivated and constitutionally defective.

---

## The Richmond Court of Inquiry (November 1838)

Following the surrender of Far West and Smith's arrest, King presided over the preliminary hearing that would determine whether the Mormon leaders should be held for trial:

**Charges against Smith:** Treason, murder, arson, burglary, robbery, and larceny.

**Conduct of the hearing (Mormon accounts):**
- Allowed anti-Mormon testimony freely while blocking Mormon counter-testimony
- Permitted witnesses to testify who had clear personal and political grievances against the Saints
- Operated in an atmosphere of armed hostility — militia present throughout
- Applied a clearly lower evidentiary standard to prosecution witnesses than to defense witnesses

**Outcome:** Smith and companions committed to Liberty Jail in Clay County to await a grand jury.

---

## Later Career

The Richmond Court of Inquiry was far from the end of King's public career:
- Elected **Governor of Missouri** (1848–1852) — suggesting his handling of the Mormon cases cost him nothing politically in Missouri
- Served in the **U.S. House of Representatives** (1863–1865)

His comfortable post-1838 career illustrates how thoroughly anti-Mormon action was rewarded rather than penalized in Missouri political life.

---

## Assessment

King is not typically portrayed as a cartoonish villain — he operated within the norms of frontier Missouri jurisprudence, which gave broad discretion to judges and was deeply sensitive to popular sentiment. But his hearing was structurally designed to commit rather than to fairly evaluate, and the outcome — months of unconstitutional imprisonment for men who were eventually allowed to escape without trial — reflects a profound failure of the judicial system.
