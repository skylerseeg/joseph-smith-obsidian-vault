# 👤 John S. Reed

**Tags:** #person/ally #era/new-york #role/lawyer
**Born:** ~1785
**Died:** Unknown
**Occupation:** Attorney, South Bainbridge, New York

---

## Summary

John S. Reed was one of Joseph Smith's defense attorneys at the **1826 Bainbridge trial** — the glass-looking or money-digging examination before Justice Albert Neely in South Bainbridge, New York. This trial, held when Smith was twenty years old, is the earliest known legal proceeding involving Smith and one of the most contested events in early Mormon history. Reed agreed to represent Smith without pay, later claiming he was moved by Smith's apparent innocence and sincerity.

---

## The 1826 Trial

Smith was brought before Justice Neely on a complaint by Josiah Stowell's nephew, who alleged Smith had defrauded Stowell by pretending to find buried treasure using a seer stone. The charge was being a "disorderly person" — a vagrancy-type offense under New York law.

Reed served as Smith's defense attorney. He later gave an account (published decades afterward) in which he described Smith as composed and persuasive before the court, and the charge as essentially a family dispute between Stowell (who fully supported Smith) and his nephew (who did not).

The outcome of the trial is disputed — some sources say Smith was convicted and fined, others that he was acquitted or the case was dismissed.

---

## Historical Significance

The 1826 trial is significant because:
1. It is the earliest documentary evidence of Smith's treasure-seeking activities
2. It predates the Book of Mormon by three years
3. Anti-Mormon writers have used it to argue Smith was a convicted fraud; LDS historians dispute the conviction
4. Reed's later published account is one of the few pro-Smith testimonies about the event
