# ⚖️ Nathaniel Pope

**Tags:** #person/ally #role/lawyer #era/nauvoo
**Born:** January 5, 1784, Louisville, Kentucky
**Died:** January 22, 1850, St. Louis, Missouri
**Occupation:** U.S. District Judge, Illinois (1819–1850)

---

## Summary

Nathaniel Pope was the federal district judge who presided over Joseph Smith's most important legal victory — the January 1843 Springfield extradition hearing — and ruled in Smith's favor, freeing him from Missouri's attempt to extradite him as an accessory to the attempted assassination of former Governor [[Lilburn W. Boggs]].

---

## The 1843 Springfield Extradition Hearing

**Background:** Missouri Governor [[Thomas Reynolds]] requested the extradition of Smith, alleging he had conspired with Orrin Porter Rockwell to assassinate Boggs.

**Defense team:** [[Justin Butterfield]] (U.S. Attorney for Illinois) and [[Orville H. Browning]]

**Core legal question:** Could Missouri extradite Smith when Smith was in Illinois — not Missouri — at the time of the alleged crime?

**Pope's ruling:** No. The extradition statutes required the accused to be a "fugitive from justice" — meaning someone who was physically present in the demanding state at the time of the offense and then fled. Since Smith had been in Nauvoo, Illinois, when the Boggs shooting occurred in Independence, Missouri, he was not a "fugitive" under the legal definition.

**Outcome:** Smith was freed.

---

## Significance of the Ruling

Pope's ruling was constitutionally sound and protected Smith by applying the law as written rather than yielding to political pressure. This was not a given — the extradition attempt had significant political backing, and the temptation to simply hand Smith over to Missouri rather than engage with the technical legal arguments must have been considerable.

The ruling bought Smith approximately 18 months of freedom before the *Nauvoo Expositor* crisis brought his final legal confrontation.

---

## Character

Pope had been on the federal bench since 1819 — nearly 25 years at the time of the Smith hearing. His long tenure gave him the institutional security to rule according to law rather than political preference.
