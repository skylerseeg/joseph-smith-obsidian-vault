# ⚖️ Josiah Lamborn

**Tags:** #role/lawyer #era/nauvoo
**Occupation:** Attorney General of Illinois
**Died:** 1847

---

## Summary

Josiah Lamborn was the Illinois Attorney General who prosecuted both the extradition hearings against Smith and — after Smith's murder — the 1845 Carthage Conspiracy Trial. His prosecution of Smith's killers was widely regarded as inadequate, culminating in his extraordinary decision to effectively disavow his own star witnesses during closing arguments.

---

## Prosecution of Smith (Extradition Hearings)

Lamborn appeared on the prosecution side during the 1843 extradition hearing — arguing in favor of Missouri's right to extradite Smith for the Boggs assassination conspiracy. He lost to [[Justin Butterfield]]'s constitutional argument before Judge [[Nathaniel Pope]].

This created an unusual dynamic: the state's top prosecutor had previously tried to hand Smith to Missouri, and was now expected to prosecute Smith's killers — men who shared much of his constituency's political outlook.

---

## The 1845 Carthage Conspiracy Trial

Lamborn served as lead prosecutor in *People v. Levi Williams et al.* — one of the most challenging prosecution assignments of the antebellum period:

**Structural obstacles:**
- No reliable eyewitnesses willing to testify (LDS survivors boycotted)
- Witness perjury from those who did testify (Franklin A. Worrell's "obvious perjury")
- Jury pool contaminated by years of [[Thomas C. Sharp]]'s editorials
- Armed partisans in the courtroom
- Defense represented by [[Orville H. Browning]] — one of Illinois's best lawyers

**The closing argument collapse:**
The most remarkable moment of the trial came when Lamborn essentially disavowed his own key witness, William M. Daniels, during closing arguments — apparently concluding that Daniels's testimony (including the "pillar of light" episode) was so incredible it would hurt rather than help his case.

Disavowing your own witness in closing argument is an extraordinary act of prosecutorial self-sabotage, but it reflects the impossible position Lamborn was in: his best witness was either lying or had seen something no secular jury could accept.

**Verdict:** Not guilty, all defendants.

---

## Death

Lamborn died in 1847, two years after the trial — a relatively obscure end for the state's chief prosecutor during one of its most consequential criminal proceedings.

---

## Assessment

Lamborn is a tragic figure in the Carthage story. He appears to have recognized that he could not win but was constitutionally required to prosecute. The disavowal of his own witness suggests a lawyer who understood the evidence was insufficient but had no better alternative to offer.
