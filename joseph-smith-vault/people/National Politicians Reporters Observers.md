## The "Five Refusals" — Smith's Petition to National Leaders

In 1843–1844, Smith formally petitioned five national political figures for support:

| Politician | Position | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Martin Van Buren | Former President | "Can do nothing for you" |
| John C. Calhoun | Senator | Federal government lacks power |
| Henry Clay | Senator, Whig candidate | Refused to commit |
| Lewis Cass | Senator | Executive lacks power |
| Richard M. Johnson | Former Vice President | Non-committal |

All five refused. Smith's response: run for president himself. The 1844 campaign was explicitly framed as a protest against this collective failure of national leadership to address religious persecution.

## The Quincy–Adams Visit (May 14–15, 1844)

The visit of [[Josiah Quincy]] and Charles Francis Adams precisely 45 days before Smith's death is one of the most documented encounters between Smith and the American elite.

**What they saw:**
- A private militia of 3,000 men
- A city of 15,000+ people built from swamp in five years
- A prophet-mayor-general who quoted Shakespeare, discussed theology in multiple frameworks, and managed complex municipal governance
- The full performance of Smith's multilayered public persona

**Quincy's verdict:** "A rare human being" whose long-term historical influence would be extraordinary — a judgment that has aged well.

## The Sam Houston Diplomacy

The Council of Fifty's negotiations with Sam Houston about a potential Mormon settlement in the Republic of Texas represents Smith's most concrete quasi-diplomatic activity:
- Smith sent emissaries to Houston
- Houston was interested — Texas needed settlers
- The talks were exploratory; nothing was finalized before Smith's death
- [[Brigham Young]] eventually pivoted to the Utah Basin instead
