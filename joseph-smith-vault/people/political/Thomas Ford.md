# 🏛️ Thomas Ford

**Tags:** #person/foe #role/politician #era/nauvoo
**Born:** December 5, 1800, Uniontown, Pennsylvania
**Died:** November 3, 1850, Peoria, Illinois
**Occupation:** Lawyer, Judge, Governor of Illinois (1842–1846)

---

## Summary

Thomas Ford was the Governor of Illinois during the final crisis of Smith's life. A conscientious but ultimately ineffectual executive, Ford promised Smith protection at Carthage, then left the scene — placing Smith at the mercy of the militia units he had just reviewed. He spent the rest of his life defending his handling of the Nauvoo crisis in his *History of Illinois* (1854).

---

## The Nauvoo Crisis (1844)

When the *Nauvoo Expositor* was destroyed on June 10, 1844, Ford was immediately drawn into the constitutional crisis:
- Non-Mormons in Hancock County demanded the state act against Smith for suppressing a free press
- Ford had to balance the rule of law against the threat of civil war in western Illinois
- He declared Smith and the Nauvoo City Council guilty of illegal conduct in ordering the press destruction

**Ford's demand:** Smith and the council must submit to civil authority and stand trial in Carthage.

**Smith's response:** Briefly considered fleeing to Iowa or the West; ultimately surrendered, reportedly saying "I am going like a lamb to the slaughter."

---

## The Broken Promise

Ford personally promised Smith protection at Carthage. On June 27, 1844, Ford left Carthage for Nauvoo to address the Mormon population directly — taking most of the reliable troops with him and leaving Smith in the custody of the **Carthage Greys**, a militia unit with documented anti-Mormon bias.

Hours after Ford left, the mob attacked.

---

## Ford's Self-Assessment

Ford was haunted by his failure. In his *History of Illinois* (published posthumously, 1854), he:
- Acknowledged that he had failed to provide adequate protection
- Argued he could not have foreseen the assassination
- Criticized the "theocratic" abuse of the Nauvoo Charter
- Described Smith as a genuine phenomenon — remarkable but dangerous to civil order

---

## Assessment

Ford is not typically characterized as a villain in the Carthage story — most historians view him as a politician overwhelmed by a crisis he could not control, making decisions that proved catastrophically wrong. He lacked either the will or the means to place Smith under reliable protection in a county that was almost uniformly hostile to Mormon survival.
