# 🏛️ Martin Van Buren

**Tags:** #person/foe #role/politician #era/nauvoo
**Born:** December 5, 1782, Kinderhook, New York
**Died:** July 24, 1862, Kinderhook, New York
**Occupation:** 8th President of the United States (1837–1841), Lawyer

---

## Summary

Martin Van Buren's famous dismissal of Joseph Smith's petition for redress — "Your cause is just, but I can do nothing for you" — became one of the most cited lines in Mormon political history. The meeting crystallized Smith's understanding that federal executive power would not intervene to protect Mormon civil rights against state persecution.

---

## The Washington Visit (November–December 1839)

Smith traveled to Washington, D.C., in the fall of 1839 with [[Sidney Rigdon]] and Elias Higbee to petition the federal government for redress of the Missouri expulsion. The Saints had suffered catastrophic losses — thousands driven from their homes, property destroyed or confiscated, leaders imprisoned — in clear violation of constitutional protections.

The delegation sought:
1. Direct federal compensation for property losses
2. Federal intervention to compel Missouri to restore citizenship rights
3. A hearing before Congress

---

## The Meeting with Van Buren

Van Buren received Smith and heard his case. His response was blunt:

> "Your cause is just, but I can do nothing for you."

When pressed for reasons, Van Buren reportedly explained that assisting the Mormons would cost him the electoral votes of Missouri and other Western states.

This was not diplomatic evasion — it was an honest statement of political calculus.

---

## Smith's Response

Smith was furious. He wrote of Van Buren with contempt in his journal and correspondence, calling him a man who sacrificed justice for political survival. Smith vowed he would never vote for Van Buren or the Democratic Party again — a pledge documented in [[David Nye White]]'s 1843 interview.

The Van Buren meeting directly informed Smith's decision to run for president in 1844 — a campaign explicitly designed to force the question of religious liberty onto the national agenda.

---

## Theological-Political Significance

The Van Buren meeting represents the moment the "states' rights" doctrine was exposed, from Smith's perspective, as a structural mechanism for religious persecution. If the federal government could not intervene when a state expelled thousands of citizens for their religion, then religious liberty under the Constitution was a fiction for minorities without political power.

This analysis drove the **Council of Fifty's** quasi-diplomatic activities — seeking arrangements with foreign nations (Texas, Mexico, Canada) because American constitutional protection had proved illusory.
