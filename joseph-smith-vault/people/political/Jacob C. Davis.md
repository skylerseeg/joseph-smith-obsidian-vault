# 🏛️ Jacob C. Davis

**Tags:** #person/foe #role/politician #era/nauvoo
**Born:** c. 1813
**Died:** unknown
**Occupation:** State Senator (Illinois), Commander, Warsaw Cadets
**Education:** William and Mary College
**Trial:** Defendant, *People v. Levi Williams et al.* (1845) — acquitted

---

## Summary

Jacob C. Davis presents the most politically complex case among the five Carthage defendants. A William and Mary-educated state senator who initially won election *with* Mormon support, Davis's turn against Smith was directly triggered by a personal political betrayal — the Mormons' refusal to back his 1844 Congressional campaign.

---

## Political Background

Davis was 31 years old at the time of the trial — a young, educated, and ambitious politician:
- **State Senator, Illinois** — a position of real political power
- **Commander, Warsaw Cadets** — militia leadership role in Hancock County
- Graduate of **William and Mary College** — unusually well-educated for the frontier Illinois political class

---

## The Mormon Alliance and Its Collapse

Davis was elected to the Illinois State Senate in **1842 with Mormon support** — the Mormon bloc in Hancock County was large enough to determine elections, and Davis initially cultivated that relationship.

The alliance collapsed in **1844** when Davis sought the Mormon vote for a seat in the U.S. Congress and Smith's community declined to support him. This personal rejection transformed Davis from a political ally into an outspoken opponent of the church.

**Key insight from [[The Media the Law and the Prophet]]:** Davis's trajectory illustrates that anti-Mormon violence in Hancock County was not purely ideological — it was often driven by concrete political and economic grievances that the Mormon bloc's unified voting created.

---

## Role in the Assassination

Davis, as commander of the Warsaw Cadets, participated in the march toward Carthage Jail on June 27, 1844. Witness **George Walker** testified that Davis refused to join the actual march to "kill a man in prison" — suggesting some ambivalence — though the prosecution disputed this characterization.

---

## The 1845 Trial

**Prosecution witness George Walker** provided testimony suggesting Davis distanced himself from the final assault. This ambiguity, combined with the broader structural problems of the prosecution, contributed to his acquittal.

**Verdict:** Not guilty, May 30, 1845.
