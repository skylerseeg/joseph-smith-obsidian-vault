# 🏛️ Stephen A. Douglas

**Tags:** #person/foe #role/politician #era/nauvoo
**Born:** April 23, 1813, Brandon, Vermont
**Died:** June 3, 1861, Chicago, Illinois
**Occupation:** Lawyer, Judge, U.S. Senator (Illinois), Presidential Candidate
**Party:** Democrat

---

## Summary

Stephen A. Douglas is the paradigmatic case in Smith's network of **political expediency as betrayal**. He was an early, genuine ally who provided real legal and political assistance to the Mormons in Illinois — and then publicly turned against the church when Mormon political influence became a liability in his broader electoral ambitions.

---

## The Early Alliance (1839–1842)

Douglas arrived in Illinois in 1833 and quickly became one of the state's most powerful Democratic politicians. When Smith and the Saints arrived in Illinois from Missouri in 1839, Douglas:
- Was a member of the Illinois Supreme Court
- "Immediately befriended" Smith — described in [[The Prophetic Circle]] as seeking the Mormon bloc vote
- Helped facilitate legal protections for the Saints in their early Illinois years
- Was personally present for some of the extradition hearings and expressed sympathy for Smith's legal predicament

---

## The Mormon Vote as Asset

The Mormon community in Nauvoo grew rapidly to an estimated 12,000–15,000 people by 1844 — a massive and **unified** voting bloc in a state where elections were often decided by small margins. Douglas's Democratic Party actively courted this vote.

---

## The Turn

As the "Mormon problem" — plural marriage, theocratic governance, the Nauvoo Legion, the *Nauvoo Expositor* crisis — became a national controversy, alignment with the Mormons became a political liability.

By 1857, Douglas had completely reversed his position, declaring in a Senate speech that the LDS church was a "disgusting ulcer" on the body politic that needed to be cut out. He supported federal action against the Utah Territory.

---

## Smith's Prophecy (1843)

In August 1843, Smith reportedly told a crowd in Nauvoo that Douglas would one day aspire to the presidency but fail — and that his failure would be connected to his treatment of the Saints. Douglas ran for president in 1860 and lost to Abraham Lincoln.

LDS historians point to this as a prophetic fulfillment; secular historians note it was a reasonable political prediction given the slavery controversy that dominated 1850s politics.

---

## Historical Significance

Douglas's trajectory illustrates the core vulnerability of Smith's political strategy: building alliances with politicians who had no principled commitment to religious liberty, only electoral calculation. When the calculation changed, the alliance dissolved.
