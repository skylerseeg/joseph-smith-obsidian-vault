## The *Maid of Iowa* Steamboat

The church's steamboat operation on the Mississippi River was one of its most ambitious commercial ventures:
- Used to transport converts arriving from Britain via New Orleans
- Managed jointly between Peter Haws, George Miller, and others
- The **Robert E. Lee connection**: The future Confederate general, then a young U.S. Army lieutenant assigned to river engineering, handled paperwork related to the sale of the *Nauvoo* steamboat — a footnote in both men's histories

---

## The Trustee-in-Trust Problem

Smith held land as **"trustee-in-trust"** — nominally on behalf of the church, but personally liable for debts:

**What this meant in practice:**
- Any debt incurred by church-affiliated mercantile firms (like Cahoon, Carter & Co.) could be pursued against Smith personally
- Bankruptcy litigation was a constant threat from 1842 forward
- Non-Mormon creditors could use the courts to reach church assets through Smith's personal liability

This structure created a legal vulnerability that Missouri and Illinois courts could exploit — and that bankruptcy petitioners did exploit throughout the Nauvoo period.

## Amos Davis — "Secular Friction" Personified

Amos Davis operated a competing mercantile business in Nauvoo. His situation illustrates the economic paradox of the Mormon presence:

**Davis benefited from:**
- Population boom driving general commerce
- Infrastructure development increasing land values
- Consumer demand from 15,000+ residents

**Davis resented:**
- Church-directed purchasing that favored Mormon merchants
- Smith's municipal court issuing favorable rulings to Mormon parties
- Theocratic control of economic life that undercut secular competition

Davis litigated against Smith repeatedly — the "secular friction" of a market participant who wanted the population benefits without the theocratic market controls.

---

## The Isaac Galland Land Deal

When Smith and the Saints arrived in Illinois from Missouri in 1839, they were refugees with no land:
- Isaac Galland offered land in Commerce, Illinois (later renamed Nauvoo) and in Iowa Territory
- The price was favorable; the terms involved complex credit arrangements
- Galland's motives were partly speculative — he held extensive marginal land that Mormon settlement would develop
- The Commerce purchase became the foundation of Nauvoo

The debt from these initial land purchases contributed to the bankruptcy pressures Smith faced throughout the Nauvoo period.
