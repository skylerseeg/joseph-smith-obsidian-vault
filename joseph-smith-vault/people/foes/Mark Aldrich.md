# 👤 Mark Aldrich

**Tags:** #person/foe #era/nauvoo
**Occupation:** Land Developer, Militia Battalion Commander
**Trial:** Defendant, *People v. Levi Williams et al.* (1845) — acquitted

---

## Summary

Mark Aldrich was a land developer and militia commander who participated in the Carthage Jail attack on June 27, 1844. His motivations were significantly economic — he had personal land grievances against the Mormon community's influence on Hancock County real estate and commerce.

---

## Background

Aldrich was a land speculator in the Warsaw/Carthage area who had invested in Hancock County real estate before and during the Mormon settlement boom. The Mormon presence — which created its own internal economic structures and directed commerce toward church-affiliated businesses — disrupted the speculative plans of non-Mormon developers who had anticipated controlling the county's growth.

His role as a **militia battalion commander** gave him military organization experience and a legitimate reason to be in the area with armed men.

---

## Role in the Assassination

Aldrich participated in the organization and march toward Carthage Jail on June 27, 1844. His exact role in the final assault is less clearly documented than that of [[Levi Williams]] or [[Thomas C. Sharp]].

---

## The 1845 Trial

**Verdict:** Not guilty, May 30, 1845.

Aldrich's land grievances are significant context for understanding the Carthage conspiracy as something more than religious bigotry — it was also a land and commerce dispute between a non-Mormon speculator class and an inward-looking religious economy that directed its purchasing power internally.

---

## Economic Dimension of Anti-Mormonism

Aldrich's profile supports the thesis in [[The Prophetic Circle]] that the opposition to Smith in Illinois was not purely ideological:

> "Non-Mormon merchants like Amos Davis represented a 'secular friction' in Nauvoo; they were beneficiaries of the Mormon-driven population boom but were legally hostile to Smith's attempt to regulate the local market through theocratic oversight."

Aldrich's land speculation grievances add a real estate dimension to this commercial friction.
