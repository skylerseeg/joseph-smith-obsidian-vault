# 👤 Isaac Hale

**Tags:** #person/foe #role/family #era/new-york
**Born:** April 21, 1763, Windham, Connecticut
**Died:** January 11, 1839, Harmony, Pennsylvania
**Occupation:** Farmer, Hunter

---

## Summary

Isaac Hale was Emma Smith's father and one of the earliest, most persistent skeptics of Joseph Smith's character and claims. His opposition was rooted in direct personal observation of Smith's treasure-seeking activities — he had seen Smith operate up close, working for [[Josiah Stowell]] near Hale's Harmony, Pennsylvania farm — and he never changed his assessment.

---

## First Impressions (1825)

Smith first encountered Hale in 1825 when he came to the Harmony area as part of [[Josiah Stowell]]'s silver mine expedition. Hale allowed Smith to board at his farm — an ordinary frontier hospitality arrangement — but quickly formed negative opinions:

- Found Smith's claims about a seer stone unconvincing
- Objected to the treasure-seeking enterprise as fraudulent
- Observed Smith's interactions with his daughter Emma with alarm

---

## Opposition to the Marriage

When Smith began courting Emma, Hale was strongly opposed:
- Considered Smith an untrustworthy "money-digger" without legitimate occupation
- Refused his consent to the marriage
- Smith and Emma eloped on January 17, 1827, without Hale's blessing

---

## Ongoing Skepticism

Hale's opposition did not soften after the marriage:
- When Smith brought the plates to Harmony in 1828 to conduct the translation, Hale asked to see them directly; Smith said they could not be shown
- Hale's published statement (1834) recounted his skepticism about the plates and the translation process
- He regarded the Book of Mormon as a product of fraud and his daughter's situation as unfortunate

---

## The Published Statement (1834)

Hale provided a signed affidavit to [[Eber D. Howe]]'s *Mormonism Unvailed* (1834) — making him one of the earliest family member critics on record. His statement was significant because of his direct relationship with Smith.

---

## Assessment

Hale represents the "proximate skeptic" — someone who had genuinely close observation of Smith's claims and remained unconvinced. His opposition was not second-hand hostility but first-person evaluation. LDS historians emphasize that Hale was never shown the plates and could not have evaluated the translation; critics see his skepticism as the reasonable response of a practical frontier farmer to extraordinary supernatural claims.
