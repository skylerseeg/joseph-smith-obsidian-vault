# 👤 Josiah Stowell

**Tags:** #person/ally #era/new-york
**Born:** March 17, 1770, Canaan, Connecticut
**Died:** May 12, 1844, Bainbridge, New York
**Occupation:** Farmer, Business Owner

---

## Summary

Josiah Stowell was Joseph Smith's first documented employer and one of his earliest supporters outside the family. A prosperous farmer in the Bainbridge, New York area, Stowell hired the teenage Smith in 1825 to help locate a rumored buried Spanish silver mine in Harmony, Pennsylvania — an engagement that led directly to Smith meeting [[Emma Hale Smith]] and to his first legal proceeding.

---

## The Spanish Mine Expedition (1825)

Stowell hired Smith based on his reputation as a "seer" capable of locating buried treasure using a seer stone. The expedition to Harmony, Pennsylvania searched for a legendary Spanish silver mine without success.

During this period, Smith boarded with the Hale family — where he met Emma Hale, whom he would marry in 1827 over her father [[Isaac Hale]]'s strong objections.

---

## The 1826 Trial

When the treasure-seeking expedition proved unsuccessful, Stowell's nephew **Peter Bridgeman** filed a complaint against Smith as a "disorderly person" or "glasslooker" — accusing him of defrauding his uncle.

At the trial in South Bainbridge, New York (March 1826):
- **Stowell himself testified in Smith's defense**, stating he believed in Smith's abilities and had not been defrauded
- Other witnesses (Arad Stowell, Jonathan Thompson) testified about the seer stone use
- The outcome is disputed: Smith was either acquitted or informally released

Stowell's testimony on Smith's behalf — against the complaint of his own nephew — reflects genuine loyalty and belief.

---

## Conversion

Stowell eventually joined the LDS church and remained a supporter. He died in 1844 — the same year as Smith — never recanting his positive view of Smith's character.

---

## Significance

The Stowell relationship establishes the **folk magic context** essential to understanding Smith's early career. Smith was not operating in a vacuum of credulity — treasure-seeking was a legitimate if marginal subculture of early 19th-century rural America, and Stowell was a respected member of his community who engaged in it.
