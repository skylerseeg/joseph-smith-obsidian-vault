# 👤 Lyman Wight

**Tags:** #person/ally #role/apostle #era/missouri #era/nauvoo
**Born:** May 9, 1796, Fairfield, New York
**Died:** March 31, 1858, Dexter, Texas
**Nickname:** "The Wild Ram of the Mountain"
**Occupation:** Farmer, Apostle, Colonel (Mormon Militia)

---

## Summary

Lyman Wight was the most militarily aggressive of Smith's apostles — a fierce frontier fighter nicknamed "The Wild Ram of the Mountain" who participated in nearly every armed confrontation of the Missouri period and spent time in [[Liberty Jail]] with Smith. After the martyrdom, he refused to follow [[Brigham Young]] to Utah, instead leading a small colony to Texas.

---

## Missouri Period

Wight was central to the Mormon military effort in Missouri:
- Commanded forces at **Adam-ondi-Ahman** (Diahman), a major Mormon settlement in Daviess County
- Known for aggressive, confrontational responses to anti-Mormon militia movements
- Helped lead the Danite paramilitary operations during the 1838 conflict
- Arrested alongside Smith after the surrender of Far West
- Imprisoned in [[Liberty Jail]] — one of the five men who shared Smith's cell

His combative style was both an asset (deterring some attacks) and a liability (escalating conflicts that might have been defused diplomatically).

---

## Apostleship

Wight was called to the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles in 1841. He remained loyal to Smith through the Nauvoo period and was a member of the **Council of Fifty**.

---

## The Texas Colony

After Smith's death, Wight refused to submit to [[Brigham Young]]'s authority and was eventually disfellowshipped. He led a group of approximately 150 followers to Texas, establishing several short-lived colonies:
- Zodiac (1847)
- Fredericksburg area settlements (1850s)

None of the colonies achieved permanence. Wight died in Dexter, Texas, in 1858 — the last of the original apostles to die outside the mainstream LDS church.

---

## Character

Wight was beloved by Smith, who reportedly called him one of the most fearless men he had ever known. His independence and refusal to subordinate his judgment to institutional authority — an asset in Missouri — became a fatal flaw in the post-martyrdom succession crisis.
