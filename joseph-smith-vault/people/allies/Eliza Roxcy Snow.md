## Summary

Eliza Roxcy Snow was the most intellectually distinguished of Joseph Smith's plural wives — a poet of genuine talent, a theologian, and eventually the second president of the Relief Society under Brigham Young. Known as "Zion's Poetess," she was one of the most prominent women in 19th-century Mormonism.

---

## Background

Snow came from a highly educated New England family. She was already a published poet before joining the LDS church in 1835. Her literary credentials were exceptional by the standards of frontier religious communities.

---

## The Sealing

Sealed to Smith on **June 29, 1842** — the same date as [[Sarah Kingsley Cleveland]], who was simultaneously serving as first counselor in the Relief Society under [[Emma Hale Smith]].

Snow served as a plural wife throughout the Nauvoo period while living outwardly as a boarder in the Smith household. [[Emma Hale Smith]] was reportedly unaware of the sealing at the time.

---

## Poetry and Literary Legacy

Snow's poetry gave the early LDS movement much of its cultural vocabulary:
- **"O My Father"** (1845) — one of the most theologically significant hymns in LDS history, introducing the concept of a "Heavenly Mother" into LDS theology
- Dozens of other poems documenting the Missouri expulsion, Nauvoo life, and the Utah exodus
- Her poetry served as a documentary record and an emotional processing of the Saints' collective trauma

---

## After the Martyrdom

Following Smith's death, Snow was sealed to [[Brigham Young]] as a plural wife — she became part of his household and continued her literary and organizational work in Utah.

She served as:
- **Second President of the Relief Society** (1866–1887) — the longest-serving president in the organization's history
- **President of the Retrenchment Association** (predecessor to the Young Women's organization)
- A central figure in Utah women's political activism, supporting women's suffrage

---

## Theological Contribution

Snow's hymn "O My Father" is credited with introducing the doctrine of a **Heavenly Mother** into mainstream LDS theology — a concept that has become one of the most distinctive elements of LDS theological anthropology and continues to be discussed and developed by LDS theologians.
