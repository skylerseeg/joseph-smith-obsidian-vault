# 📜 Warren Parrish

**Tags:** #person/foe #role/scribe #era/ohio
**Born:** January 10, 1803, New York
**Died:** January 3, 1877
**Occupation:** Scribe, Bank Officer, Minister

---

## Summary

Warren Parrish is one of the clearest examples of the "scribal liability" pattern in Smith's career — a trusted insider whose access to private documents and financial records made his defection uniquely dangerous. He served as Smith's personal secretary and the Kirtland Safety Society's teller before becoming one of its most vocal critics.

---

## Rise as Scribe (1833–1837)

Parrish replaced [[Orson Hyde]] as Smith's scribe and served in that role through the critical mid-1830s period:
- Transcribed journals and correspondence
- Worked closely with Smith on the "History of the Church" project
- Served as a teller (bank clerk) for the **Kirtland Safety Society**

---

## The Banking Crisis (1837)

When the Kirtland Safety Society failed in 1837, Parrish was one of the most prominent apostates produced by that disaster:
- Led a dissident faction within the Kirtland church
- Accused Smith of knowing the Safety Society was illegal and proceeding anyway
- Took members of the dissident "Parrish faction" into various Protestant churches
- Charged Smith with "lying and extortion"

The Parrish defection was particularly dangerous because, as a former scribe and bank officer, he had:
1. Detailed knowledge of the Safety Society's inner workings
2. Access to financial records that could be used as evidence
3. Credibility as a recent intimate of Smith's leadership

---

## Departure from Mormonism

Parrish eventually became a minister in mainstream Protestant denominations, leaving Mormonism entirely rather than joining a splinter group. He later served Congregationalist and other Protestant congregations.

---

## The Pattern

Parrish exemplifies what [[The Prophetic Circle]] identifies as the core vulnerability of Smith's network:

> "The proximity to 'prophetic power' carried a high cognitive and social burden, particularly when spiritual claims clashed with practical economic failures."

When the Safety Society collapsed, the gap between Smith's prophetic claims and his financial decisions became intolerable for men like Parrish who had seen both up close.
