# 📜 William Clayton

**Tags:** #person/ally #role/scribe #era/nauvoo
**Born:** July 17, 1814, Penwortham, Lancashire, England
**Died:** December 4, 1879, Salt Lake City, Utah
**Occupation:** Accountant, Clerk

---

## Summary

William Clayton was the indispensable financial and administrative clerk of Joseph Smith's final years. He managed Smith's personal accounts, kept the minutes of the secret **Council of Fifty**, and recorded the exact date of the plural marriage revelation — giving him more sensitive documentary knowledge than almost any other person in Nauvoo.

---

## Arrival and Rise

Clayton immigrated from England to Nauvoo in 1840 with a group of British converts. His accounting skills and organizational precision made him indispensable:
- Appointed clerk to the **Red Brick Store** — Smith's commercial and administrative headquarters
- Appointed clerk to the **Council of Fifty** — the secret political body
- Managed Smith's financial records during the most complex period of Nauvoo's growth

---

## The Plural Marriage Revelation

Clayton was present on **May 1, 1843**, when Smith dictated the plural marriage revelation (now Doctrine & Covenants 132). Clayton recorded it in full. He later testified that [[Hyrum Smith]] took the document to show [[Emma Hale Smith]], who reportedly burned her copy.

Clayton's journal entry on that date is one of the primary documentary evidences for the revelation's existence and timing.

---

## Personal Practice

Clayton himself entered plural marriage in Nauvoo, which gave him both personal investment in the doctrine's preservation and personal vulnerability if it were exposed.

---

## "Come, Come, Ye Saints"

Clayton wrote the iconic Mormon pioneer hymn "Come, Come, Ye Saints" during the 1846 exodus from Nauvoo to Utah — one of the most enduring cultural artifacts of the early LDS movement.

---

## Documentary Legacy

Clayton's journals and Council of Fifty minutes are among the most sensitive and sought-after documents in Mormon history. They were kept confidential for over a century and only recently made available through the **Joseph Smith Papers Project**.
