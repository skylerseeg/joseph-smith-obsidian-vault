# 💰 Economics MOC — Smith's Business and Financial Networks

---

## Core Economic Model

Smith operated a **communal-private hybrid model**:
- Held land as "trustee-in-trust" for the Church
- Was personally liable for debts incurred by mercantile firms
- Non-Mormon merchants were beneficiaries of the population boom but legally hostile to his theocratic market regulation

---

## The United Firm (1832–1834)

A joint-stock business partnership managing the Church's temporal interests in Ohio and Missouri. Dissolved under the pressures of the Missouri conflicts.

**Key partners:** [[Newel K. Whitney]], [[Sidney Rigdon]], [[Frederick G. Williams]]

---

## The Kirtland Safety Society (1837)

Smith's most consequential economic failure.

**Context:** Land prices near Kirtland rose from $7 to $44/acre between 1830 and 1836, driven by a flood of converts (1,000 → 3,000 members). Smith attempted to monetize this land value through a banking institution.

**The problem:** The Ohio legislature — influenced by political foes like [[Grandison Newell]] — refused a bank charter. The "Anti-Banking Company" became an illegal entity.

**Result:** Massive financial losses for Saints who had invested. Loyal apostles transformed into internal dissenters.

**Key dissenters created:** [[Warren Parrish]], Luke Johnson, Lyman E. Johnson, John F. Boynton

## The Bankruptcy Problem (1842–1844)

The threat of "bankruptcy litigation" haunted Smith from 1842 until his death. Non-Mormon merchants like Amos Davis represented a "secular friction" — they benefited from the Mormon-driven population boom but sued Smith repeatedly over market regulation disputes.

---

## Robert E. Lee Connection

An early-career Robert E. Lee (then a U.S. Army Lieutenant) handled the sale of the *Nauvoo* steamboat — an obscure but documented intersection with the Mormon story.

---

## Second-Order Economic Insights

1. **Kirtland failure** → Radicalized insider apostles into dissenters (Cowdery, Parrish, etc.)
2. **Nauvoo land boom** → Non-Mormon merchants embedded in the economy created "secular friction" and legal hostility
3. **Trustee-in-trust model** → Personal liability for corporate debts created constant legal vulnerability
4. **Economic collapse = social collapse** — The pattern repeated: banking crisis in Ohio, land conflicts in Missouri, debt litigation in Illinois
