# 💰 Kirtland Safety Society

**Tags:** #theme/economics #era/ohio
**Organized:** November 1836
**Collapsed:** 1837
**Location:** Kirtland, Geauga County, Ohio

---

## Summary

The Kirtland Safety Society was Joseph Smith's most consequential economic venture and his most catastrophic failure. Organized as a banking institution to monetize the rapid land appreciation around Kirtland, it failed within months of opening — taking the savings of hundreds of Saints with it, triggering a wave of apostasies, and forcing Smith to flee Ohio in January 1838.

---

## Context: The Kirtland Land Bubble

The rapid conversion of Sidney Rigdon's congregation and subsequent waves of converts caused an extraordinary demographic and economic transformation in Kirtland, Ohio:

- Church membership: approximately **1,000 → 3,000** between 1830 and 1836
- Land prices: **$7 → $44 per acre** — a 6x increase driven by Mormon settlement demand
- A flood of converts brought capital, labor, and commercial demand into a small rural community

Smith recognized that the church sat on enormous latent land value. The Safety Society was designed to monetize that value through a banking institution that could issue notes backed by Kirtland real estate.

---

## The Charter Problem

Smith and Sidney Rigdon applied to the **Ohio state legislature** for a bank charter in late 1836. The application was denied.

The political context: [[Grandison Newell]] and other opponents in the Ohio commercial community lobbied against the charter. The Jacksonian era's hostility to "monster banks" made any new charter politically difficult.

**Smith's response:** Proceed anyway as the **"Kirtland Safety Society Anti-Banking Company"** — a quasi-bank issuing notes without a charter.

This was, under Ohio law, an **illegal operation**.

---

## The Collapse

The Safety Society issued notes that almost immediately began trading at a discount to face value. Several factors accelerated the collapse:
- Notes were not backed by sufficient specie (gold/silver)
- The broader Panic of 1837 — a national banking crisis — hit simultaneously
- Note holders began demanding redemption; the Society could not meet demands
- Confidence evaporated; notes became worthless

Losses fell disproportionately on ordinary Saints who had invested their savings. Estimates suggest the total losses ran into the tens of thousands of dollars.

---

## The Apostasy Wave

The Safety Society failure produced the most severe internal rupture in the church's history to that point. Men who had been among Smith's closest associates turned against him:

| Apostate | Former Role |
|---|---|
| [[Warren Parrish]] | Smith's personal scribe; bank teller |
| Luke Johnson | Apostle |
| Lyman E. Johnson | Apostle |
| John F. Boynton | Apostle |
| [[Oliver Cowdery]] | "Second Elder"; co-founder |
| [[Martin Harris]] | Original financial patron |
| Hazen Aldrich | Leader of the Seventies |

---

## Smith's Response

Faced with arrest on illegal banking charges (prosecution led by [[Grandison Newell]]), Smith and [[Sidney Rigdon]] fled Kirtland in January 1838, traveling to Far West, Missouri, at night to avoid creditors and legal process.

---

## Second-Order Consequences

1. The apostasy wave removed a generation of original leadership — men who had known Smith since 1829–1830
2. The Missouri exodus concentrated the church population in an area of intense anti-Mormon sentiment
3. The "unilateral decision-making in temporal affairs" criticism that followed Smith through Nauvoo was born here
4. Former apostates like Parrish and Cowdery took their documentary knowledge with them
