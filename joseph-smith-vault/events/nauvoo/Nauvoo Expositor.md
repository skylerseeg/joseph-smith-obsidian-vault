# 📰 Nauvoo Expositor

**Published:** June 7, 1844 (single issue)
**Location:** Nauvoo, Illinois
**Publisher/Editor:** [[Sylvester Emmons]] (editor), Law brothers and associates
**Destroyed:** June 10, 1844 — by order of Smith and the Nauvoo City Council
**Tags:** #theme/media #theme/law #era/nauvoo

---

## Overview

The *Nauvoo Expositor* was a dissident newspaper published by former insiders of Joseph Smith's inner circle. Its single issue on June 7, 1844 detonated a chain of events that ended in Smith's assassination 20 days later.

---

## The Publishers

These were not traditional "anti-Mormons" but **Reformers** — men who wished to return to what they considered the original, pure doctrines of the church:

| Name | Role | Primary Grievance |
|---|---|---|
| [[William Law]] | Former Second Counselor, First Presidency | Learned of polygamy; viewed Smith as a "fallen prophet" |
| [[Wilson Law]] | William's brother; Nauvoo Legion General | Organized the Reformed Mormon Church |
| [[Robert D. Foster]] | Physician; former associate | Claimed Smith attempted to seduce his wife |
| Charles A. Foster | Publisher | Robert's brother |
| [[Francis M. Higbee]] | Attorney | Filed sworn statement; sued Smith for slander |
| [[Chauncey L. Higbee]] | Lawyer/Judge | Excommunicated for adultery in 1842 |
| Charles Ivins | Publisher | Member of the reform group |
| [[Austin Cowles]] | Former High Council member | Resigned in protest of polygamy revelation |
| [[Sylvester Emmons]] | Editor | City Councilor; former ally |

---

## Content of the Sole Issue

The paper alleged:
1. **Plural marriage was being secretly practiced** — backed by insider testimony
2. **Theocratic abuse of the Nauvoo Charter** — Smith using city powers for religious ends
3. **The Council of Fifty** — existence of a secret political kingdom-building body
4. **Smith was a "fallen prophet"** — not that the church was false, but that its leader had gone astray

---

## Smith's Response: The Destruction of the Press

On June 10, 1844 — three days after publication — the Nauvoo City Council declared the *Expositor* a "public nuisance." Smith, as mayor, ordered the city marshal to destroy the press, type, and all remaining copies.

**Legal reasoning at the time:** Municipal nuisance doctrine allowed cities to abate threats to public peace.

**Perception from outside Nauvoo:** This was an act of tyranny — a sitting government official destroying a free press.

### The "Media-Legal Cycle" Trigger

From [[The Media the Law and the Prophet]]: The press destruction was the single event that converted regional anti-Mormon sentiment into a statewide political crisis, giving [[Thomas C. Sharp]] and his allies the moral high ground they needed to frame Smith's assassination as a defensive act.

---

## Legal Consequences

1. Riot charge filed by Constable William Bettisworth
2. Charge escalated to **treason** by Illinois Governor [[Thomas Ford]]
3. Smith surrendered to Carthage on June 24–25, 1844
4. Assassinated June 27, 1844

---

## Historical Significance

The *Expositor* affair illustrates the central paradox of Smith's Nauvoo leadership:

> His attempt to suppress the *Expositor* was legally defensible under 1840s municipal law but was politically catastrophic — it handed his opponents the "tyranny" narrative they needed to justify violence.
