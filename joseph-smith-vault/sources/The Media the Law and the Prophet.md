# 📄 The Media, the Law, and the Prophet — Research Paper Summary

**Full Title:** The Media, the Law, and the Prophet: A Socio-Legal Analysis of Joseph Smith Jr. and the Frontier Press
**Type:** Gemini Deep Research Paper

**Date Loaded:** 2025

---

## Thesis

The relationship between Smith and the journalists who chronicled his life was "not merely reflective but constitutive" of the social and political crises that led to his assassination. The press and legal system of the frontier era were structurally unable to balance religious freedom against a perceived theocratic threat.

---

## The "Media-Legal Cycle"

The central mechanism of this paper:

> Press shapes public opinion → Public opinion shapes jury pools and mob action → Legal outcomes reflect the media atmosphere, not the evidence.

| Stage | Press Actor | Legal Result |
|---|---|---|
| New York (1830s) | [[Abner Cole]] mockery | 1830 "disorderly person" trials |
| Ohio (1834) | [[Eber D. Howe]] / Hurlbut affidavits | National stereotype, persistent for decades |
| Illinois (1844) | [[Thomas C. Sharp]] violent editorials | Murder, and subsequent acquittal of killers |

## Three Reporter Categories

### 1. Neutral-to-Favorable National Press
- **[[David Nye White]]** — Pittsburgh Weekly Gazette, 1843 interview. Recorded First Vision. Called Smith's eloquence "amazing volubility."
- **[[James Gordon Bennett]]** — NY Herald. Smith called his coverage "fair" and "high-minded."

### 2. Local Investigative Critics
- **[[Abner Cole]]** — Palmyra Reflector. Pseudonym "Obadiah Dogberry." Earliest journalistic critic of the Book of Mormon.
- **[[Thomas C. Sharp]]** — Warsaw Signal. Primary inciter. Violent editorials turned local disputes into statewide crisis.

### 3. Internal Apostate Reformers
- **[[Sylvester Emmons]]** — Edited the sole issue of the *Nauvoo Expositor*.
- **[[William Law]]** — Former First Presidency counselor turned publisher.

## Legal Timeline Summary

- **1826** — South Bainbridge, NY. "Disorderly person / glasslooker" trial. [[Josiah Stowell]] testified in his defense.
- **1834** — Painesville, OH. Smith vs. Hurlbut for death threats.
- **1838–1839** — Missouri. Treason and murder charges. Liberty Jail.
- **1843** — Springfield, IL. Extradition hearing before Judge [[Nathaniel Pope]]. [[Justin Butterfield]] wins: can't extradite for a crime committed in another state.
- **1844** — Nauvoo. *Expositor* destruction → riot charge → treason. Carthage Jail.
- **1845** — *People v. Levi Williams*. All defendants acquitted.

Smith was involved in approximately **220 legal cases** as plaintiff, defendant, witness, or judge.

## The 1845 Carthage Conspiracy Trial

**Core finding:** "Deliberate political assassination" involving prominent local leaders.

### Defendants
| Name | Role | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| [[Thomas C. Sharp]] | Editor, Warsaw Signal | Primary organizer of Anti-Mormon Political Party |
| [[Levi Williams]] | Baptist minister, Col. 59th Militia | Allegedly ordered the charge on the jail |
| [[Mark Aldrich]] | Land developer, militia commander | Personal land grievances |
| [[Jacob C. Davis]] | State Senator | Turned after Mormons denied him Congressional backing |
| [[William N. Grover]] | Captain, Warsaw Rifle Co. | Reportedly bragged about the killing |

**Verdict:** Not guilty, all defendants. May 30, 1845.

**Why acquittal was inevitable:** Sharp's press had "lionized the assassins as defenders of liberty," making an impartial jury in Hancock County impossible.

---

## Synthesis
- The failure of the 1845 trial was a *direct* product of the media atmosphere Sharp created.
- Mormon belief confirmed: "they would never find safety under the laws of the United States."
- This accelerated the exodus to the Great Basin.
